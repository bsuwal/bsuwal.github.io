I am an enthusiastic student of the history and culture of Nepal broadly and the Kathmandu Valley specifically.

This interest has been motivated (unsurprisingly) to a large extent by a natural curiosity and desire to understand my own heritage and culture better. In this I feel very lucky to have the privilege of knowing my roots. However there are two other purely academic reasons why I love reading about Nepal, both a byproduct of its unique history:

- Nepal was closed off to the rest of the world until relatively recently (1951), after which it underwent a very rapid modernization. This has created the very interesting situation where within a single life span (of say, my grandmother) Nepal has come from what in Europe could be considered as life in the 1600s to the present day. In doing so I think the immensely rapid cultural evolution of Nepal has made for a uniquely interesting society.
- Until relatively recently, Nepal has had a strong oral tradition of passing down stories and customs. I therefore get great joy from written accounts of traditions/history that have been and are rapidly being lost to time.

Some themes that I have been interested in:
* Situations where rapid modernization experienced friction with entrenched social tradition.
* The lives of the Lhasa Newar traders who traded between Lhasa and Kathmandu for centuries.
* Old maps and pictures of Kathmandu and Nepal broadly.
* Vestigial/Anachronistic customs still part of the fabric of Kathmandu.
* Early accounts of weather, wildlife and flora, mostly to compare to what is observed today.
* The social structures of the population prior to 1950
* The lore of the Kumari
