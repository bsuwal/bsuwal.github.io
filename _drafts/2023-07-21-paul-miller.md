I stumbled upon this story of a guy named Paul Miller, who in 2012 spent a year off the internet in New York City.
He writes about it here and talks about it here. Interestingly, he recently published a 10 year reflection on his experiment on his blog.

All of it was very fascinating to me, and I will highlight particular details in the end, and lead this post with the major things.

To me it seems like a big lesson he learnt was that we are cyborgs, whether we want it or not. Our online and offline lives sum up to become our lives. At the centre of it all was the conflict of how much do we wish to spend in one place vs the other.

Indeed, during his offline year he met Nathan Jurgenson, author of The Social Photo, and it seems that that had left a lasting impression on his thoughts of what it means to be live, without sounding too dramatic. (Disclaimer: I did learn of Paul and his experiment through The Social Photo, so there is an echo chamber here. But Jurgenson has convinced me of his theory of dualism, so this is more of a note than an echo chamber disclaimer.)

Most people could not experiment the way Paul did for a year, and therefore I hold his reflections with a lot of weight. He did feel isolated, and in his 10 year update he said he still believed that his life was richer because of the people he had met through the internet. This was instructive to read. When choosing to forego? these social media platforms, I have known that connections that could be made are being missed out on.

What I did feel a bit lost on, was I wished he had expanded a bit more on the tradeoffs of being online. He clearly felt strongly enough about how the internet was getting in the way of how he thought his life should be, and after his experiment he says his conclusion is that it was not the internet that was getting in the way, but other habits/issues of his.

So is the internet completely forgiven of its blame? I would have liked to ask that. In his video, when he described how the internet was a tool for his parents while for him the internet was where his life was, I think there was an understanding there that while the internet can be horrible with its distractions, it can also be a great thing, if used as just a tool. It seemed to me that if Paul could do that, then he would be happy. It was an admission from him that it was not like that to him, and because of his social network's activity and conversation spaces, this divorcing was very tough for him. This is probably a good endorsement for the Lightphone. WE will see.

Aside: what do I wish to gain from the Lightphone? I know that being gone from the internet is unsustainable. I will need maps, I will need to look stuff up, I will need to read articles. I think what I wish to gain is more downtime. That in and of itself is already a win. The opportunity to be bored feels like a good thing for me. ahile nai hera na, the fact that i am writing this here is time I would rather spend here than on a phone. It might not last forever, but I am excited for this experiment. Also, I realized that a lot of times I am thinking of work when I am walking around, and developing the proof further, so when I do sit down to write something the idea is clearer in my head. That is a major win.

While I would never do anything like that, I definitely was interested in what he had to say. Here are my thoughts:

Things of note:
- He thought of being on the internet as an unnatural state for us humans.
- At the beginning, he did feel great. His attention span did expand.
- "I have no trouble navigating New York by feel." "It turns out paper books are really great." "I don't comparison shop to buy plane tickets, I just call Delta and take what they offer."
- He got a PO Box?? Crazy idea.
- "I abandoned my positive offline habits, and discovered new offline vices." This was by late 2012, so about month 6-7.
- He did start playing video games though, and listening to audio books.
- "So the moral choices aren't very different without the internet."
- "When I stopped seeing my life in the context of 'I dont use the internet', the offline existence became mundane and the worst sides of myself began to emerge."
- "I can tell you a Facebook Friend is better than nothing."
- He met Nathan Jurgenson who said that online and offline lives are intertwined. It seems to have made an impact on him. I will be writing about him soon too.
- "What I do know is that I can't blame the internet, or any other circumstance, for my problems."
- "I might waste time, or get distracted, or click on all the wrong links. I won't have as much time to read or introspect or write the great American sci-fi novel. But atleast I will be connected."

Then I watched his video, which continues the story a bit farther after he wrote the article.

He said he missed an entire year's worth of cute moments with his nephew and niece, and he realized it during a Skype call. This is a real issue, that I have grappled with myself.

And then his 10 year retrospection.

He is back on the internet. He still believes that it is easier to connect with people when you are plugged in. That I have no doubt about. The loss of connections that could be is a very real thing in a world where being on the internet is the default.
