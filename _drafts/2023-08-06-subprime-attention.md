While I have spent much time meditating and reading about the effects of addictive screens, I had only a rudimentary understanding of the capitalist economics of why this technology was being pushed to us in the first place. Digital advertising is much of what makes the attention wheel go round, and reading Tim Huang's Subprime Attention Crisis helped me this landscape better.

I had two major takeaways from the book that I was pretty convinced by:

1. Advertising, online or not, suffers from a core problem that has haunted it since the ages - it is hard to pin down exactly how effective it is.

footnote:
Does advertising work? That seems to be the first question to answer. As a concept it is hard to argue against. I will not seek a product or a service if I haven't heard of it.


The best unit of success would be if a person purchases a product after seeing an ad that they would not have purchased otherwise. This seems like a very difficult thing to measure. And while this seems a good unit of success at the individual ad level, it seems to me that success from advertising often comes from being served repeated reminders of a product - may be it is the 14th ad that finally convinces me. Or I might never think of needing an esoteric item until the day I do, and then I purchase it from the one firm who has reminded me of its existence all along. Either way, it seems that unless one has access to the detailed workings of the purchaser's mind, it seems like a hard thing to truly learn.

2. The markets that serve this online advertising space are increasingly opaque and speculative. I found the arguments on this topic the most interesting, and I will elaborate them below.

The book's argument was that these two in conjunction make the digital advertising industry ripe for a financial bubble. Nothing good can come out of a situation where there is so much speculative, opaque trade over commodities of uncertain worth. I feel less convinced by this stance.

One of the best parts of the book was to explain the algorithmic online market.

Consider you are a seller of rice in your village. You know all fifteen of your customers, and you go to their houses everyday to sell grain to them. The villagers know you well and trust your grain and therefore are happy purchasing from you.

Now imagine that your village gets connected to a major city. Now you have more people you can sell to. You travel to the city to sell your rice, but you cannot go to each prospective customer's house, there are too many of them. And they don't know you either, so they are skeptical of your claim that your rice is of the quality you claim it to be. This is a problem of scaling trust.

A solution is to have a trusted intermediary. When you bring your grain to this intermediary, it will assign it a quality grade, say Grade A. Then your grain gets mixed with all the other Grade A grain in its stockpile. When the customers come to this intermediary, their purchase might contain grain from many other sellers. "Can we measure human attention in a simple and reliable way? Can we break human attention down into discrete units that can be exchanged in the marketplace?"
The advertising industry is deeply invested in answering these questions in the affirmative.

IAB guideline to what a viewable impression is: https://www.iab.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/MRC-Viewable-Ad-Impression-Measurement-Guideline.pdf

Fascinating. "Viewable Display Ad Impressions are
counted when the following criteria are met:
• Pixel Requirement: Greater than or equal to 50% of the pixels in the
advertisement were on an in-focus browser tab on the viewable space of
the browser page, and
• Time Requirement: The time the pixel requirement is met was greater
than or equal to one continuous second, post ad render."

It is now possible to purchase attention in marketplaces without knowing where and how that attention was produced. Since the system is so opaque, the quality might be deteriorating and we will never know.

Who benefits by the opacity? The middlemen. Google and FB.

These brands benefit by the opacity and can misreport their numbers. They had some controversies and after a lot of pressure can get accredited now about the metrics they advertise to the advertisers. However, the power dynamic remains the same apparently - they get to choose which third party platform can do this. I, the prosecuted, get to choose my judge.
